### What is the Computer Science NEA?

In case you haven't started, or maybe it's simply in the back of your head, let me quickly explain what you might expect this coming year in your Computer Science A-level. 

The NEA (Non-examined Assessment) is simply one major coding project you tackle throughout year 13. You come up with an idea such as a video game, a website or system that you will both document the process of building and implement in real life.

The NEA has 5 main sections.
- Analysis - Describing the problem you are trying to solve, and how you will solve it.
- Design - Diagrams and descriptions of your plans as to how you will implement your project.
- Implementation - The actual code itself.
- Testing - Displaying how you tested your project works, and fixed anywhere that it may break.
- Evaluation - How did your project go? Did it meet all requirements? Are you happy with it? What could have gone better?

By writing out these sections, and completing your code - your NEA will be complete! 

## Main Advice for Completing your NEA

### Plan for Algorithms, Not Content

This is, in my opinion, the most important advice I can give on the NEA. Your priority is to get as high of a mark as possible, and therefore, you should be aiming to come up with ideas or add features which will boost your marks, not just because it's cool. To give you an idea of what I mean, say you are making a video game. Making 1-2 weapons is fantastic, it is a necessity for you game, and will obviously fulfill requirements. However making 5, 6, 8, 10, weapons is excessive and won't improve your marks any further than having just 1 or 2. Why? Because you don't add any new complex algorithms or code this way, it's simply the same thing repeated in slightly different ways. Don't get me wrong, it is cool to add more content to your game, and could make it more fun in the future, however your priority now is to get good marks on your NEA, not to publish a game (focus on that afterwards!).

Instead you should focus on what features will include a new complex algorithm, a new data structure, a database, or inheritance (OOP). You should by now have learnt so many more complicated computer science concepts which are absolute mark goldmines for your project. For instance, Trees and Graphs. Making the data structures themselves is complex, but even better is that you can code dijkstras, A*, depth- and breadth-first search algorithms. These are amazing for the complexity marks within your implementation. Not only this, but you have to plan these algorithms (in your Design section) and you also have to extensively test them (in your testing section). That is amazing content for 3/5 parts of your NEA! All from just coming up with a feature that includes, for example, a Graph and A* algorithm. 

This also goes for things like databases - do you need to store lots of information about users and other objects in your project? OOP is another fantastic one to plan for, think how you could include inheritance, association, etc in your project (those buzzwords are what markers are looking for!).

One final note, do not overcomplicate things for no reason. The idea of this is to come up with new features that require these complex ideas. If you just have users and a leaderboard, you probably don't need an entire relational database! A simply file read-write system will do just fine (and still score you marks!). Focus on new features with the intent of including one of these data structures or algorithms (or better, both!).

### Designing Should be Continuous

I am aware that the NEA is extremely structured, one section after the other. However, designing and planning your project is impossible to complete the first time round. Once you start researching and programming further, you may realise a more efficient method of doing something, you may stumble across a must-have feature that includes a new algorithm/data structure. These require more planning!

The bulk of your planning should be done at the beginning. It makes life so much easier when you begin to code if you know exactly what features you have to implement, and how to do it. This will all come in the form of  pseudocode, flowcharts, UML diagrams, database diagrams, etc that you make during your design stage. However this is never fixed, the examiners only ever see your final document. You can tinker with you design whenever you need - which you should! You should be editing and adding things to improve those marks as much as you can!

My general advice on approaching the design would be design your overall system and must-have algorithms first, before you start coding. This will give you an idea as to how your project glues together, how to implement the main features and makes a good starting point. As you get going, any extra or unaccounted for features that come up, you should then go back to planning and include it. Your aim is to plan as much as you can before implementing anything in order to prevent you from overscoping, having unmanageable code and getting stuck.

### Don't Overscope

A year might sound like a long time, however amongst balancing all your over A-levels and trying to optimise for marks, it goes around quicker than you might expect. Because of this, you need to be careful not to over-do your project requirements. Overscoping is where you add so many features that it is simply impossible to complete them in time. 

This often reveals itself in two ways : 
1. Getting overly ambitious at the beginning with your idea.
2. Constantly thinking up new ideas as you go.

Don't get me wrong, both of these are so fun to do, and often come from the excitement of building your project. However, it can quickly turn into burn-out and motivation loss if you get caught up in features that are not necessarily required for you project as a whole.

To prevent this, you need to look at your project as an MVP (Minimum Viable Product). What are the bare minimum project requirements that I HAVE TO implement in order for my project to be complete. Think about what features actually matter, and which ones simply would just be fun or helpful. By reducing your project down to its skeleton, it will give you a clear path of what you need to accomplish, and it will also save you tonnes of time working on features that don't matter. 

By doing this, not only do you ensure you can complete your project in time, but also opens you up to finishing early. Once you finish early, you now are able to either focus on everything else (other A-levels, exam prep, whatever it may be) or you can add one, two, three extra features that you were so desperate to do beforehand (prioritise those algorithms and data structures!). 

### Start early!
Similar to before, the aim is to give yourself plenty of time to complete your project, and give you more time to add and tinker with your documentation to boost marks. 

The absolute easiest way to do this, is to start early. Start during summer, start at the latest in September (when school begins again).

Honestly there is not much more to add to this, simply expand the amount of time you get to work on it, as it's better to finish early, than to fit all your coding and documentation into 3 months of late nights!


## Conclusion

Just to outline the main points of today's talk :
1. Add features rich in algorithms and data structures, not just content for your project.
2. Keep designing during your coding stage.
3. Don't over-scope your project, keep it to an MVP.
4. Start your project as early as possible.

By following these main steps, or just keeping them in mind whilst you plan and make your project, you will have a far better time during this next year and also hopefully increase your marks by having more rich features in your project.
